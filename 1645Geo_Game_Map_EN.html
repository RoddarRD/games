IV. Historical Context

17th-Century French Cartography & the Making of Le Jeu du Monde

Pierre Du Val & the Paris Cartographic Workshop

Born in Abbeville in 1619, Pierre Du Val was the nephew and protégé of Nicolas Sanson, widely regarded as the founder of modern French cartography. Publishing from his Paris shop on the rue Saint-Jacques à l’Espérance under royal privilege (Privilége du Roy, 1645), Du Val pioneered geographical education through compact atlases, wall tables, and pedagogical board games.

At just twenty-six years old, Du Val dedicated Le Jeu du Monde to Louis Victor de Rochechouart, Comte de Vivonne, First Gentleman of the King’s Chamber—whose heraldic arms adorn the center of the engraved sheet. Today only two copies of this 51 × 40 cm 1645 edition are known to survive: one at the Bibliothèque nationale de France and one in the David Rumsey Historical Map Collection.

Play as Geography: Adapting the Game of the Goose

To make global geography memorable for aristocratic youths and polite salons, Du Val fused cartography with the 63-space spiral of the Jeu de l’Oie (Game of the Goose). Players cast two dice to travel inward from the farthest periphery of European exploration toward Paris, encountering oceanic straits, trade winds, quarantines, and shipwrecks along the way.

“The object of the game is to progress from the outer parts of the world in towards the center—France.”

Two miniature inset hemisphere maps in the upper corners anchored the spiral visually, while restrained hand-applied color washes separated continents and kingdoms on the copperplate paper.

California as an Island & Eurocentric Cosmology

Le Jeu du Monde is a time capsule of mid-17th-century geographical certainty and illusion. In Circle 5, California appears unmistakably as a detached island off New Spain—an error born from misread 1602 Pacific accounts and popular 1620s London and Amsterdam engravings that French cartographers would not fully abandon until Father Eusebio Kino’s overland surveys after 1701.

Equally revealing is the game’s geometry: placing France at Circle 63 casts Europe as the gravitational goal of human navigation. Non-European continents fill the outer turns, whereas European principalities expand to nearly one-third of all spaces as the spiral tightens toward the French capital.
